## Fukuyama's Position

`[FUKUYAMA]` Accountability exists when rulers understand themselves as responsible to governed interests. It can be moral, institutional, or electoral; modern representative government formalizes and enforces it, but does not exhaust the category.

## Competing Interpretations

A narrower theory reserves political accountability for answerability plus an external capacity to sanction. On that account, a ruler's inward moral responsibility is benevolence or trusteeship, not accountability.

## Philosophical Analysis

Fukuyama uses a graded family-resemblance concept: moral orientation is the weakest form, organized social constraint an intermediate form, and electoral removal the strongest modern form. This supplies comparative breadth but risks conceptual overextension.

## Phase 2 Findings

`[FUKUYAMA]` Accountability means rulers regard themselves as responsible to governed people and place public interests above their own (ch. 22, PDF pp. 339–340). He allows moral education as one form and treats organized representation and elections as stronger formal forms. `[INTERPRETATION]` Accountability is broader than democracy, but the distinction between internal responsibility and enforceable accountability must remain visible in analysis.

## Closure Adversarial Finding

`[VOLUME II]` Chapter 1 explicitly distinguishes substantive responsiveness from procedural accountability (PDF pp. 26–28), and chapter 25 distinguishes Chinese responsiveness from downward accountability (pp. 400–403). Bovens's actor–forum account and Schedler's answerability/enforcement distinction provide a narrower external comparator. The sanction criticism therefore survives only as a qualified Level 3 objection: Fukuyama's breadth reduces precision, but it is deliberate rather than an internal oversight.
