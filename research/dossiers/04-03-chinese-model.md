## Fukuyama's Position

`[FUKUYAMA]` Zhou lineage-based rule was transformed under Warring States pressure into centralized territorial administration. Qin intensified Legalist techniques; Han preserved much of the administrative structure while supplying Confucian legitimation. China therefore developed a strong bureaucratic state without autonomous rule of law or institutional accountability.

## Historical Evidence

`[SCHOLAR]` Early territorial centralization and bureaucratic techniques are supported. `[CONTESTED]` Whether Qin/Han already constituted a mature “modern state,” and whether warfare is a sufficient explanation, remains disputed. `[SCHOLAR]` Han administration coexisted with elite families and later capture, qualifying any clean transition to impersonality.

## Competing Interpretations

One reading treats Qin/Han as an exceptionally early administrative state. A stricter chronology locates mature bureaucracy later and treats unification, hegemony, elite restructuring, migration, and ideology as co-causes rather than effects of war alone.

## Philosophical Analysis

The term “modern” is definition-dependent. Under Fukuyama's narrow administrative criterion—centralized, territorial, comparatively impersonal bureaucracy—the China-first claim is defensible. Under a constitutional or sociologically fuller conception of modernity, it is misleading. The dossier must therefore separate institutional priority from civilizational superiority or comprehensive modernity.
