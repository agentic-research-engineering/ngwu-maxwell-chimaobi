# Conceptual Framework

Each entry records a provisional distinction, not a substitute for page-specific textual research. Locations are chapter-level until verified against stable printed pagination.

| Concept | Provisional Fukuyama usage | Distinction or ambiguity | Primary route |
|---|---|---|---|
| Political order | Stable, legitimate institutional organization capable of governing | Not identical to democracy or moral justice | *Origins* chs. 1, 29–30 |
| Political development | Change in political institutions, analytically separate from economic/social development | May involve improvement without linear inevitability | *Origins* preface, chs. 29–30; *Decay* chs. 1–2 |
| Political decay | Institutions fail to adapt or are captured/repatrimonialized | Not merely regime collapse | *Origins* chs. 1, 9, 15, 29; *Decay* pt. IV |
| State | Centralized, hierarchical organization claiming legitimate coercive authority over territory | State is not government, nation, democracy, or mere capacity | *Origins* ch. 5; *Decay* intro |
| State capacity | Quality/ability with which government functions are executed | Distinct from state scope (the functions attempted); a capable state may be illiberal or unaccountable | *Decay* ch. 2, canonical PDF p. 62; chs. 1–3, 13 |
| State building | Historical/political construction of state institutions | Process, not achieved condition | *Origins* pt. II |
| Institution | Stable, valued, recurring rule or pattern organizing behavior | Formal versus informal scope requires verification | *Origins* ch. 29 |
| Institutionalization | Acquisition of stability, value, autonomy, and rule-boundedness | Huntington's influence requires direct comparison | *Origins* preface/ch. 29 |
| Patrimonialism | Public authority treated as extension of ruler/household and personal ties | Distinguish patrimonial administration from ordinary corruption | *Origins* chs. 9, 23–26 |
| Neopatrimonialism | Outwardly modern bureaucracy, law, and elections combined with rule for private gain | Distinguish from classical patrimonialism and from all corruption | *Decay* ch. 1, canonical PDF p. 29 |
| Repatrimonialization | Impersonal institutions captured again by personal/familial/elite interests | Central to decay; scope differs across volumes | *Origins* ch. 15; *Decay* ch. 33 |
| Kinship | Biologically/culturally organized relations generating solidarity and obligation | Kinship is not simply tribalism | *Origins* chs. 2–4, 6, 10, 16 |
| Tribalism | Segmentary organization through extended kin groups/lineages | Avoid using as a timeless civilizational label | *Origins* chs. 3–4 |
| Tyranny of cousins | Pressure of kin obligations against impersonal institutions | Metaphor requires contextual interpretation | *Origins* ch. 3 |
| Bureaucracy | Hierarchical administration, ideally selected and governed impersonally | Bureaucracy alone does not equal modern state | *Origins* chs. 7–9; *Decay* ch. 3 |
| Impersonal administration | Office/rules take priority over personal and kin ties | An ideal-type and developmental achievement, not absence of interests | *Origins* chs. 7–9, 29 |
| Legalism | Chinese political doctrine/practice strengthening centralized authority | Not synonymous with rule of law | *Origins* chs. 7–8 |
| Rule of law | Binding body of law understood as superior to the ruler | Distinguish from rule by law: rulers using law instrumentally | *Origins* chs. 17–21 |
| Political accountability | Rulers answer to organized social actors/interests | Broader than electoral democracy | *Origins* chs. 22–28 |
| Legitimacy | Recognized rightfulness of authority | Empirical acceptance and normative justification may diverge | *Origins* chs. 1, 5, 29 `[VERIFY]` |
| Human nature | Evolved dispositions supporting kin selection, reciprocity, norm-following, and recognition | Does not mechanically determine institutions | *Origins* chs. 2, 29 |
| Natural sociability | Humans begin socially embedded rather than as isolated presocial individuals | Compare carefully with each contract thinker | *Origins* ch. 2 |
| Reciprocal altruism | Cooperation based on repeated reciprocal benefit | Distinct from kin selection | *Origins* ch. 2 |
| Kin selection | Cooperation biased toward genetic relatives | Biological mechanism does not exhaust cultural kinship | *Origins* ch. 2 |
| Recognition | Desire for acknowledgment of dignity/status | Hegelian genealogy and causal role need verification | *Origins* ch. 2 |
| State of nature | Philosophical device contrasted with evolutionary/anthropological reconstruction | Fukuyama's account is not simply another contract fiction | *Origins* ch. 2 |
| Historical contingency | Outcomes depend on sequences, shocks, and choices not necessity alone | Tension with general developmental theory | *Origins* chs. 29–30 |
| Institutional sequencing | Order in which state, law, and accountability emerge | Sequence affects later balance and pathologies | *Origins* pts. II–IV, ch. 28 |
| Path dependence | Early arrangements constrain later options | Constraint is not determinism | *Origins* preface/ch. 29 `[VERIFY]` |
| Universalism | Claim that institutional functions or aspirations have broad applicability | Distinguish universal criteria from one Western route | *Origins* chs. 1, 30 |
| Historical determinism | Necessitarian or teleological reading of development | Must test rather than presume; Fukuyama stresses multiple paths | `[CONTESTED]` |
| Modernization | Interrelated economic, social, and political change | Political development remains analytically autonomous | *Origins* preface/ch. 30; *Decay* chs. 1–2 |
| Getting to Denmark | Shorthand for prosperous, stable, democratic, well-governed order | Risks masking history and normative assumptions | *Origins* chs. 1, 28 |
