# Abstract

This study investigates Francis Fukuyama's explanation of the emergence, differentiation, interaction, and decay of the institutions necessary for political order, with particular attention to the state, rule of law, and political accountability. It uses philosophical reconstruction, conceptual analysis, comparison, and critical evaluation, drawing principally on *The Origins of Political Order* and its companion volume, *Political Order and Political Decay*, together with classical political philosophy and relevant specialist scholarship. The investigation finds that Fukuyama usefully distinguishes political capacity, legal constraint, and accountability as institutionally separate dimensions that need not arise together or follow one historical sequence. His comparative histories of China, India, Islamic political orders, and Western Europe highlight the importance of war, kinship, religion, law, administration, and organized social constraint while also revealing substantial variation in their operation. The study therefore rejects strict historical determinism as an adequate criticism of Fukuyama. It argues instead that his triad is most defensible as a comparative diagnostic framework for identifying institutional imbalance, adaptation, and decay. Its main limitation arises when broad functional categories, including the modern state, accountability, weakened kinship, and institutional convergence, compress historically distinct mechanisms into a common developmental framework. The study concludes that Fukuyama offers a valuable but qualified account of political order: recurrent political problems permit comparison, but historically plural mechanisms limit any universal sequence or sufficient model of political development.
