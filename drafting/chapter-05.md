# CHAPTER FIVE

# CRITICAL EVALUATION AND CONCLUSION

## 5.1 Strengths of Fukuyama's Theory of Political Order

The strongest feature of Fukuyama's theory is its refusal to identify political order with a single institutional virtue. A government may be democratic but administratively weak, legally constrained but incapable of enforcing rights, or powerful without meaningful limits on its rulers. By separating state capacity, rule of law, and political accountability, Fukuyama supplies a vocabulary for explaining these combinations rather than ranking every polity on one scale.[^1] This differentiation is philosophically important because it prevents effectiveness, legality, and responsiveness from silently substituting for one another. It also exposes genuine conflicts among political goods. The power needed to implement law may become a source of arbitrary rule; legal and political checks that prevent arbitrariness may also obstruct coordinated action. The framework is therefore valuable not because it promises harmony, but because it identifies the tensions that any adequate account of political order must confront.

This diagnostic value is strengthened by Fukuyama's historical method. Political institutions are not treated as devices that can simply be selected from an abstract menu. They are products of conflict, social organization, religious authority, inherited norms, administrative experimentation, and unintended consequences. The Chinese, Indian, Islamic, and Western European comparisons demonstrate that similar political problems may be addressed in different orders and by different mechanisms. Early Chinese rulers constructed considerable territorial and administrative capacity before autonomous law or representative accountability developed. In India, extensive states coexisted with durable normative and social orders whose authority was not reducible to royal command. Islamic military slavery reorganized recruitment and loyalty through coercive institutions that bypassed some inherited ties while creating new households. Western Europe's divided jurisdictions strengthened forms of law and corporate constraint before state consolidation was complete.[^2] These are not interchangeable routes, and Fukuyama is at his best when comparison reveals the consequences of their differences.

The historical account also improves political philosophy by placing institutions within human social life. Chapter Three showed that Fukuyama rejects both isolated individualism as a literal human beginning and biological reductionism as an adequate account of political development. Kin loyalty, reciprocity, status, norm-following, and group attachment are persistent resources of cooperation as well as pressures on public office. Institutions do not abolish these dispositions. They organize the terms under which personal obligations may influence public authority. This insight gives impersonality a more precise meaning. Impersonal administration is not the absence of social relations or human motives; it is an achievement in which office, rule, and public purpose have priority over family or client demands in specified decisions.[^3] The same anthropology helps explain why patrimonial pressures recur without making their victory inevitable.

A related strength is explanatory pluralism. Fukuyama assigns importance to warfare, religious ideas, material incentives, geography, institutional borrowing, leadership, and social structure, but he does not consistently reduce development to any one of them. War may stimulate extraction and administration without being sufficient or necessary for state formation. Religious institutions may legitimate rulers, organize law, or create autonomous authority, but religion does not operate through one political mechanism. Kinship can facilitate cooperation and administration as well as patronage and capture. This pluralism makes the theory less elegant than a single-cause model, but more responsive to the actual diversity of political formation. It also permits ideas to be causally important without detaching them from organizations and material conditions.[^4]

Fukuyama further recognizes sequencing and reversibility. State capacity, law, and accountability need not arise together, and the order of their development affects later possibilities. Yet no institutional achievement is permanently secured. Rules may become rigid when circumstances change; elites may redirect public institutions toward private advantage; and formally modern organizations may be repatrimonialized. The conceptual connection between formation and decay is a major contribution. A theory that explained only institutional origins would risk treating development as an accomplished destination. Fukuyama instead asks how the same institutions that solve coordination problems generate new forms of power, dependence, and capture.[^5]

These strengths explain why the triad should not be discarded merely because particular classifications or causal chains are disputed. Hewson's description of Fukuyama's evolutionary account as probabilistic, and Manning's characterization of the project as a middle-range effort between abstract theory and historical particularism, capture its best methodological ambition.[^6] It seeks recurring mechanisms without claiming that every society moves through one fixed series of stages. Its broad categories make institutional comparison possible; its historical narrative shows that those categories are never instantiated in a social vacuum. The critical issue is consequently not whether abstraction occurs. Every comparative theory abstracts. The issue is whether the abstraction preserves the differences needed to explain the cases being compared.

## 5.2 Critique of Fukuyama's Conception of State Institutions

The principal limitation of Fukuyama's theory appears when its functional categories are asked to carry more explanatory weight than their internal distinctions can sustain. The objection is not that China, India, Islamic political orders, or Western Europe fail to reproduce pure ideal types. Ideal types are useful precisely because historical institutions are mixed. The stronger objection asks when a category that initially clarifies a political function begins to conceal differences relevant to the explanation. Four limit tests—the breadth of accountability, the classification of Chinese modernity, the treatment of India, and the Christian kinship sequence—show why the answer must be given case by case.

Fukuyama deliberately uses accountability more broadly than theories that require an actor to provide information and justification to a forum capable of judgment and consequences. His substantive category includes rulers who understand themselves as responsible to public interests, as well as governments constrained through consultation, organized social power, or elections.[^7] This breadth has a real advantage. A definition restricted to competitive elections would miss historically important mechanisms through which rulers responded to subjects, estates, towns, churches, or organized elites. It would also encourage the false conclusion that every non-electoral order is wholly unaccountable.

The cost is reduced discrimination. Responsiveness concerns whether power attends to social demands; answerability concerns explanation and justification; sanctions concern enforceable consequences; representation concerns whose interests are institutionally present. A ruler may be responsive without having to answer to an independent forum, and may answer without facing removal or punishment. Bovens's actor–forum model makes this distinction especially clear.[^8] Fukuyama can respond that political history requires a vocabulary broad enough to capture incomplete and non-electoral constraints. That response defeats the claim that he simply forgot sanctions, but it does not show that all these relations should bear the same name. The concept remains most useful when qualified by its mechanism: substantive responsiveness, elite constraint, answerability, downward sanction, or electoral accountability. Without such disaggregation, accountability risks becoming a general term for public-regarding government.

The classification of early China presents a parallel problem. Fukuyama's description of Qin and Han China as the first modern state is coherent under a restricted Weberian criterion: centralized territorial authority administered through increasingly impersonal rules and officials recruited beyond hereditary aristocracy.[^9] Specialist evidence supports early bureaucratic monarchy, extensive registration and extraction, and a remarkable degree of administrative reach. The objection therefore cannot be that nothing institutionally distinctive occurred. Nor does the absence of liberal rights, elections, or autonomous rule of law refute a definition that does not include them.

The difficulty lies in the surplus meanings carried by the word “modern.” Administrative modernity is not constitutional, liberal, democratic, social, economic, or national modernity. Even within the administrative dimension, Qin and Han institutions retained hereditary rank, elite-family competition, personal loyalty, and uneven recruitment. Calling the system modern can isolate a real innovation while encouraging readers to import properties that the stipulated definition excludes. A well-informed Fukuyamian may reply that terminological discipline is the reader's responsibility and that “administratively modern” permits comparison across conventional period boundaries. This reply substantially limits the objection. The residual criticism is not inconsistency but classificatory risk: a provocative label may do more rhetorical work than the qualified historical criterion warrants. The defensible conclusion is that early China was administratively modern under Fukuyama's restricted criteria, not that it instantiated the wider institutional complex normally associated with political modernity.[^10]

India tests the explanatory force of civilizational contrast. Fukuyama identifies an important pattern: extensive political authority coexisted with durable religious and social rules that rulers did not wholly create or control. Yet the evidence does not justify treating India as lacking centralized states, nor does it establish Brahmanical authority or caste as the primary cause of weaker or less durable centralization. Mauryan rule was extensive but regionally uneven; royal patronage also helped constitute Brahmin privilege; and chronology, ecology, communications, war, and agrarian organization remain rival or interacting mechanisms.[^11] Fukuyama can reasonably answer that macrohistory compares dominant institutional tendencies rather than denying every exception. That defence preserves the contrast between relatively autonomous social order and Chinese state-centred rule. It does not, however, establish the direction or weight of causation. The Indian case therefore supports a comparative observation more securely than a civilizational causal hierarchy.

The Christian kinship argument exposes the same problem across a longer chain. Ecclesiastical authorities promulgated distinctive rules concerning marriage, consanguinity, inheritance, and family formation. Historical and cross-regional scholarship gives qualified support to the proposition that prolonged exposure to this programme was associated with weaker intensive kinship. It is further plausible that altered kin organization affected property, corporate life, and the environment in which impersonal institutions developed.[^12] Each step, however, introduces variation in enforcement, region, class, household form, and competing causes. Kinship persisted and changed rather than disappearing.

Fukuyama's strongest response is methodological. Macrohistory cannot demand experimental isolation for every social transformation. It must synthesize specialist findings and trace mechanisms whose effects unfold across long periods. This response is persuasive against the demand for impossible certainty. Still, the degree of confidence appropriate to an early link cannot simply be transferred to a distant political outcome. Church regulation of marriage is better established than Church-caused kinship weakening; kinship reorganization is better supported than its sufficiency for individualism or impersonal politics. Macrohistorical breadth is legitimate, but causal confidence should decrease as distance and the number of intervening mechanisms increase.

These limit tests identify a general weakness without turning the chapter into a prosecution. Fukuyama's categories continue to clarify administrative capacity, legal autonomy, social constraint, and public responsiveness. The criticisms succeed only where classification substitutes for specification or where a long sequence is narrated with the confidence appropriate to its strongest component link. They do not show that ideal types are useless or that one historical complication destroys the theory. They show that the theory must distinguish conceptual stipulation from ordinary meaning, comparative pattern from causal priority, and plausible mechanism from demonstrated chain.

A final boundary concerns normativity. State capacity, legal constraint, and accountability identify functions relevant to political order, but successful performance does not by itself settle justice, rights, democratic equality, representation, distribution, legitimate coercion, or political obligation. A capable state can pursue unjust purposes; an inherited law can be stable without being legitimate; a government can answer effectively to a narrow constituency. Fukuyama may respond, correctly, that a theory of political development need not provide a complete normative philosophy. The point is therefore a scope boundary before it is a defect. The framework explains important conditions under which public power operates, while the ends and justification of that power require additional argument. The next sections ask whether this bounded framework nevertheless directs diverse histories toward a privileged developmental endpoint.

## 5.3 The Problem of Historical Determinism

Strict historical determinism is not a fair description of Fukuyama's theory. Determinism would require political outcomes to follow necessarily from antecedent conditions or a fixed law of development. Fukuyama instead emphasizes contingency, multiple causes, different institutional sequences, borrowing, unintended consequences, unsuccessful transitions, agency, and reversibility. He states that inherited paths constrain possibilities without determining societies' futures, and his evolutionary analogy relies on variation, selection, and adaptation rather than a programmed destination.[^13] Volume II makes the point more explicit by distinguishing purposive political change from random biological mutation and by allowing both divergence and possible convergence.[^14] The claim that his theory simply makes every society pass through one necessary series of stages must therefore be rejected.

Rejecting determinism does not remove every concern generated by the inherited section heading. Determinism, teleology, functionalism, convergence, retrospective selection, and path dependence are different propositions. Path dependence means that earlier choices and structures constrain later options; it does not make those options singular. Functional explanation identifies an institution partly through the political problem it solves; it does not by itself claim that the institution had to emerge. Convergence predicts or interprets movement toward similar solutions; it can remain probabilistic. Teleology is stronger where history is organized by an endpoint that supplies direction or evaluative meaning. Retrospective selection occurs when successful cases are chosen and interpreted through the outcome they eventually achieved.

The strongest criticism concerns the interaction among the last three. Fukuyama begins from recurrent problems—coercion, scale, legal constraint, elite capture, and responsiveness—and identifies institutional arrangements that solve them comparatively well. “Getting to Denmark” condenses those arrangements into a benchmark of prosperity, stability, democracy, and effective government. The benchmark can guide diagnosis without predicting that every society will reach it. Yet it can also influence case selection and classification: histories become intelligible according to whether they produced the state, rule of law, and accountability in recognizable forms. Fukuyama's disclosed use of successful state-building cases in one comparative exercise illustrates the danger. Selection may reveal a mechanism, but it cannot by itself establish how frequently the mechanism operates or why apparently unsuccessful alternatives should be classified as failures.[^15]

Direct scholarship reinforces this bounded concern. Hewson accepts the probabilistic character of Fukuyama's biological account while questioning universal convergence and the triad's ability to capture later political innovation. Mungiu-Pippidi argues that the two-volume theory displays multiple paths but lacks a fully persuasive classification scale and evidential basis for convergence. Melleuish's accessible abstract similarly identifies a universal evolutionary model as the target of historical criticism, although the full premises of his argument have not been verified and are not attributed here.[^16] These criticisms do not demonstrate hidden necessity. They question how historical variety is organized and what warrants movement from recurring functions to a common endpoint.

Fukuyama's strongest response is substantial. Recurrent solutions to recurrent political problems do not imply that history is predetermined. Some arrangements may enable collective action, constrain predation, and correct rulers more effectively than others. Societies can discover, borrow, combine, or lose these arrangements through contingent processes. Evolutionary comparison can accordingly study variation and selection while recognizing extinction, maladaptation, and divergence. Macrohistory would lose much of its explanatory purpose if every institutional form were treated as incomparable merely because it arose in a unique context.

That response defeats the determinism charge and limits the teleology objection, but it leaves a residual question. Is the endpoint only an analytical benchmark, or does it quietly decide which historical changes count as development before their mechanisms have been assessed? The evidence supports a bounded verdict. Fukuyama is not strictly deterministic, and convergence need not mean inevitable uniformity. His framework nevertheless contains functionalist and convergence-oriented pressures that can encourage retrospective interpretation. The proper safeguard is to treat the triad as a set of comparative questions rather than a destination against which every history is ranked.

## 5.4 Universalism and Alternative Paths to Political Development

The determinism analysis permits a more exact treatment of universalism. Four claims must be separated: human societies may confront widespread political problems; some institutional functions may recur; identical institutional forms may be universally required; and all societies may follow a universal historical sequence. The first proposition is highly plausible. Problems of collective action, coercion, legitimacy, coordination, elite capture, and responsiveness arise wherever durable public authority is organized. The second is defensible at an appropriate level of abstraction: political orders need some means of acting, regulating power, and relating rulers to governed interests. Neither proposition entails universal forms or sequences.

The comparative cases establish the distinction. China developed centralized administration through warfare, territorial reform, and bureaucratic innovation without first developing autonomous rule of law. India combined large-scale rule with durable social and religious authorities whose relationship to the state varied across time and region. Islamic rulers used religious community, military slavery, and household organization to reconstruct political loyalty, with centralizing and repatrimonializing effects. Western Europe developed divided jurisdictions, corporate bodies, and legal constraints before achieving uniformly strong territorial states.[^17] These cases do not express timeless civilizational essences. They demonstrate that capacity, constraint, and responsiveness can be institutionalized through different organizations and causal sequences.

Fukuyama can defend his categories as ideal types. An ideal type is not refuted because no society perfectly instantiates it. Its purpose is to isolate a feature—administrative capacity, legal supremacy, answerability—that mixed historical cases combine in different degrees. On this view, the triad allows comparison precisely because it does not require an English parliament, a Chinese bureaucracy, or a particular ecclesiastical jurisdiction to be reproduced everywhere. The same function can be realized through more than one form.

This defence must be taken seriously. The criticism that reality is more complicated than theory is trivial unless the omitted complexity changes the explanation. The stronger test asks whether the abstraction preserves causal differences. Calling both moral responsiveness and electoral removal “accountability,” for example, may identify a shared orientation toward governed interests, but it can obscure who demands an account and what happens when rulers refuse. Calling Qin/Han China modern may identify administrative innovation while concealing forms of hereditary and personal power relevant to impersonality. Describing Church policy as kinship weakening may conceal regional variation and the difference between dismantling lineages and reorganizing family relations. In such cases, abstraction clarifies one function while risking the erasure of the mechanism that produced or limited it.

The appropriate universalism is therefore layered. Political problems may be widespread; functions can be compared; forms remain plural; sequences remain contingent. This position retains Fukuyama's most important comparative insight while rejecting the inference that similar problems require identical institutional packages. It also reframes convergence. Political orders may converge functionally in limited respects—such as developing professional administration—while diverging in legal authority, democratic control, social organization, or normative purpose. Convergence along one dimension does not establish convergence of political order as a whole.

Historical plurality consequently matters to philosophy, not only to descriptive history. Different mechanisms distribute power and responsibility differently even when they perform related functions. A bureaucracy created through military competition, a legal order sustained by religious jurisdiction, and accountability won through bargaining among organized estates each carry distinct relations of coercion, membership, and exclusion. The triad remains useful when it enables those differences to be compared. It becomes misleading when the functional label is treated as the complete explanation. Universal political problems support diagnostic comparison; they do not prove a universal institutional form or evolutionary sequence.

## 5.5 Relevance of Fukuyama's Theory to Contemporary Political Philosophy

Fukuyama's framework remains relevant because it changes the questions asked about contemporary political order. The presence of elections does not answer whether a state can implement collective decisions. Formal legality does not establish that law effectively binds incumbents. Administrative strength does not show that public power responds to the governed. The triad therefore directs inquiry beyond the single question whether a regime is democratic. It asks: can the state act, is its action legally bound, to whom must it respond, are offices administered impersonally, and can its institutions adapt without being captured?[^18]

These questions reveal several conceptual pathologies without requiring a new empirical case study. Capacity without constraint can make arbitrary policy effective. Constraint without capacity can render rights and public decisions merely declaratory. Electoral accountability without professional administration can produce mandates that the state cannot carry out. Administrative autonomy can protect expertise while weakening downward control. Legalization and veto points can prevent abuse while also making adaptation difficult. Democratic procedure can coexist with patrimonial capture when public office is allocated through personal or client relations. The value of the framework lies in distinguishing these combinations rather than assigning all of them the same diagnosis.

The theory is equally relevant to debates about institutional reform. Fukuyama's historical account warns against transferring a formal institution while ignoring the sequence and social relations that sustain it. A civil-service rule, court, election, or anti-corruption office may perform differently where administrative capacity, organized social power, and informal obligation differ. This does not prohibit institutional borrowing; Fukuyama explicitly treats borrowing and learning as sources of change. It requires reform arguments to specify mechanisms rather than infer results from institutional names.

The contemporary relevance is nevertheless bounded by the normative limit already identified. The triad does not by itself decide how much capacity is legitimate, which rights law should protect, who deserves political standing, how resources should be distributed, or what equality requires. Even the language of balance does not supply a formula for resolving conflict among effectiveness, restraint, and participation. Fukuyama can answer that institutions capable of action and correction are conditions for pursuing many normative purposes, not substitutes for those purposes. That answer is persuasive. Rights require implementation; democratic choices require administration; and public power requires limits. But necessary institutional conditions do not determine the ends for which they should be used.

The framework's enduring relevance is thus diagnostic and agenda-setting. It identifies dimensions along which political orders can fail and shows why improvement on one dimension may create pressure on another. It also directs attention to decay as maladaptation and public capture rather than only constitutional breakdown. Contemporary political philosophy gains from these distinctions while retaining responsibility for questions of justice, legitimacy, equality, and obligation that the developmental framework does not settle. The governing thesis emerges from this result: the triad remains powerful when it structures inquiry, but becomes too ambitious when treated as a universal historical destination or a complete account of political value.

## 5.6 Summary

This investigation has found that Fukuyama's most important contribution is the differentiation of political power into three problems that are too often collapsed. State capacity concerns whether public authority can act; rule of law concerns whether rulers are subject to authoritative rules; political accountability concerns the relation between rulers and those whose interests their power affects. These dimensions interact, but success in one does not guarantee success in the others. Their differentiation makes it possible to diagnose capable despotism, legally constrained weakness, ineffective democracy, elite capture, and obstructive institutional excess without treating them as one undivided failure.

The historical inquiry also established that political institutions arise from socially embedded and path-dependent processes. Human beings are naturally social, but kinship, reciprocity, norms, and status do not mechanically determine one political order. Warfare, religious authority, administration, material interests, legal organization, borrowing, and agency combine differently across cases. China, India, Islamic political orders, and Western Europe therefore illuminate alternative mechanisms and sequences rather than successive stages on a common ladder.

Several stronger formulations were rejected. The evidence does not support strict historical determinism, a necessary universal sequence, comprehensively modern ancient China, stateless India, the simple destruction of kinship by the Western Church, or the equation of every responsiveness mechanism with enforceable accountability. Nor can institutional origins or successful functions establish political legitimacy by themselves. These rejections do not empty Fukuyama's theory of content. They identify where its claims must be narrowed.

What remains defensible is substantial. The triad captures recurring functions of political order and reveals their permanent tensions. Fukuyama's historical approach explains why institutional achievements are difficult to construct, why their sequence matters, and why they remain vulnerable to rigidity and capture. His ideal types permit comparison across mixed cases. Their limit appears when a shared functional label replaces analysis of the distinct mechanism, or when a plausible sequence is presented with more confidence than its distant causal links allow.

The result is neither unqualified endorsement nor wholesale rejection. Fukuyama provides a persuasive diagnostic vocabulary and an ambitious account of institutional formation and decay. The comparison requires historical qualification, conceptual disaggregation, and independent normative judgment. Once those controls are imposed, the framework remains philosophically valuable without being accepted as a universal evolutionary map.

## 5.7 Conclusion

The central problem of this study was how Fukuyama explains the emergence, differentiation, interaction, and decay of the state, rule of law, and political accountability, and whether that explanation is philosophically adequate. The answer is qualified. Fukuyama explains political order by connecting evolved human sociality to historically produced institutions. States concentrate territorial power; autonomous law limits the immediate will of rulers; accountability relates government to organized social interests and, in its modern procedural form, to citizens capable of sanction. These institutions emerge separately, combine unevenly, and remain exposed to maladaptation and repatrimonialization.

This account is philosophically strongest as a method of diagnosis. It asks three questions that no theory of political order can safely merge: can authority act, is it bound, and to whom does it respond? The questions expose why democratic procedure cannot substitute for administrative capacity, why state strength cannot substitute for legal restraint, and why law cannot substitute for political responsiveness. They also show that institutional balance is not a final equilibrium. It is an unstable relationship requiring continuing adjustment, because each political good can correct or obstruct the others.

The framework's limits emerge from the same comparative ambition that gives it value. Administrative modernity, accountability, weakened kinship, and institutional convergence are useful abstractions, but each can gather together arrangements whose causal and normative differences matter. Early Chinese administration supports a restricted classification, not a complete modernity claim. Indian political history supports comparison between state and social authority, not a simple absence of centralization or a proven religious master cause. Church rules support a qualified kinship mechanism, not an independently demonstrated route to impersonal politics. Broad accountability preserves historical range, but must distinguish responsiveness from answerability and sanction.

Historical plurality is therefore not an inconvenience to be removed from the theory. It is evidence about how political functions are realized. Societies may confront recurring problems of capacity, constraint, coordination, legitimacy, responsiveness, and capture without resolving them through identical organizations or sequences. Ideal types clarify these problems when they isolate comparable functions; they distort when they erase differences required to explain outcomes. The appropriate conclusion is not cultural incomparability, but disciplined comparison at the level the evidence can sustain.

Fukuyama's theory is consequently not strictly deterministic. Its recognition of contingency, path dependence, agency, borrowing, multiple causes, and reversibility rules out that charge. The surviving concern is subtler: functional comparison and convergence language can orient historical diversity toward a privileged image of successful order. Treating the triad as a diagnostic framework resolves much of this tension. It allows institutions to be compared without asserting that all histories move toward one form or that performance along the three dimensions exhausts political value.

The philosophical contribution of this study is this bounded reconstruction. Political order should be evaluated through differentiated relations among effective public power, legal constraint, and responsiveness, while the historical forms of those relations remain open to plural explanation. Their emergence does not establish their justice, and their functionality does not settle rights, equality, distribution, representation, obligation, or legitimate coercion. Yet normative purposes cannot be realized securely where states cannot act, law cannot bind, or rulers cannot be corrected. Fukuyama's triad thus identifies indispensable questions without supplying every answer. Its enduring value lies not in providing a universal destination for political history, but in clarifying the institutional tensions through which political communities pursue, lose, and reconstruct order.
