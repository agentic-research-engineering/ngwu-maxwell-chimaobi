# CHAPTER THREE

# FRANCIS FUKUYAMA AND THE PHILOSOPHICAL FOUNDATIONS OF POLITICAL ORDER

## 3.1 Francis Fukuyama: A Detailed Biographical Overview

Francis Fukuyama's intellectual background helps to explain the unusual range of *The Origins of Political Order*, but it does not determine the truth of its arguments. He studied classics at Cornell University and received a doctorate in political science from Harvard University. His subsequent work included service at the RAND Corporation and on the Policy Planning Staff of the United States Department of State, followed by academic appointments at George Mason University, Johns Hopkins University, and Stanford University.[^1] These settings placed him at the intersection of political theory, comparative politics, public policy, and questions of institutional development.

Fukuyama first became widely known for *The End of History and the Last Man*, which examined liberal democracy as an ideological and political horizon. His later work increasingly addressed the institutional conditions without which liberal principles cannot be effectively realized. Publications on state-building, governance, development, and the rule of law prepared the ground for the two-volume political-order project: *The Origins of Political Order* in 2011 and *Political Order and Political Decay* in 2014.[^2] The shift is important for this study. The principal question is no longer only which regime possesses normative legitimacy, but also how states acquire capacity, how rulers become subject to law, how governments become accountable, and how these institutional achievements deteriorate. Biography is relevant here only as intellectual context. The adequacy of Fukuyama's answers must be judged by his concepts, arguments, and evidence.

## 3.2 The Concept of Political Order

Fukuyama treats political order as an institutional achievement rather than as the mere absence of open conflict. Markets, civil society, and democratic mobilization cannot by themselves perform all the functions of public authority. A political community requires institutions capable of making and enforcing collective decisions, maintaining order, and supplying public goods.[^3] This claim does not establish how extensive state action should be or whether a particular state is legitimate. It identifies the problem that any theory of political order must confront: organized public power cannot simply be assumed away.

His account distinguishes political development from economic and social development. These processes influence one another, but they do not necessarily advance together. Economic growth does not automatically produce an impartial administration; elections do not automatically create a capable state; and bureaucratic strength does not automatically subject rulers to law. Fukuyama therefore rejects explanations that reduce political development to one economic, cultural, or biological cause. Historical paths constrain later options, but they do not make one future inevitable.[^4] Political order is consequently a multidimensional problem.

The central expression of that multidimensional approach is Fukuyama's distinction among “the state,” “the rule of law,” and “accountable government.”[^5] Each institution answers a different political question. The state concerns whether public authority can act effectively across a territory. The rule of law concerns whether rulers are bound by rules that possess meaningful authority independently of their immediate wishes. Accountable government concerns whether political power responds to governed populations or to organized social actors able to demand responsibility. These functions are related, but none is simply another name for the others. A state may be administratively capable without being legally constrained. Law may restrain rulers while administrative weakness prevents reliable implementation. Political accountability may exist procedurally while the government lacks the capacity to carry out public decisions.

Fukuyama describes successful modern liberal democracy as a stable balance among the three institutions.[^6] “Balance,” however, should not be understood as effortless harmony. State capacity concentrates power, while law and accountability restrict, direct, or challenge its exercise. Excessive checking can obstruct action, whereas capacity without effective constraint can support arbitrary government. The framework's immediate value is therefore diagnostic: it prevents democracy, legality, and administrative effectiveness from being treated as interchangeable measures of good political order. Whether these three categories constitute a universally sufficient account remains a later critical question. At this stage, they provide the conceptual structure through which Fukuyama investigates institutional development.

This institutional framework rests on an account of human beings as naturally social yet persistently inclined toward kin preference, status competition, and group attachment. To understand why impersonal political institutions are both possible and difficult to sustain, it is therefore necessary to examine Fukuyama's philosophical anthropology.

## 3.3 Fukuyama's Understanding of Human Nature

Fukuyama rejects the image of human beings beginning as isolated adults who subsequently choose society. His argument is that human sociality has deep evolutionary foundations. He identifies kin selection and reciprocal altruism as two natural sources of cooperative behaviour: individuals tend to assist relatives, while repeated interaction can sustain cooperation beyond immediate genetic kin.[^7] Neither mechanism is a complete political theory. Together, however, they help explain why social organization precedes the state and why early cooperation is often structured by family and repeated personal obligation.

Fukuyama supplements these mechanisms with the human capacity to follow norms. People do not respond only to immediate material advantage; they also treat shared rules as meaningful and may enforce them against themselves and others. He further connects human status-seeking with the struggle for recognition. This allows him to explain political conduct that cannot be reduced to material calculation alone, including the defence of rank, dignity, religion, and inherited social position.[^8] The connection requires care. Evidence that status motivations have evolutionary roots does not by itself establish that Hegelian recognition is reducible to biological competition. Fukuyama's account is best read as a synthesis in which evolved dispositions, cultural norms, and historically formed ideas interact.

Contemporary research supports the broad claim that humans are an unusually cooperative and social species, but it also requires a wider range of mechanisms than a simple opposition between kin selection and reciprocity. Cooperation can depend on reputation, partner choice, punishment, signalling, learned norms, cultural transmission, and institutions. Gene–culture interaction is especially important because it explains both recurrent human capacities and substantial variation across societies.[^9] Research on hunter-gatherer residence also complicates any assumption that early social groups consisted mainly of close biological relatives. Comparative evidence from foraging societies indicates flexible residence patterns and extensive relations with affines and non-kin.[^10] Human sociality is therefore neither presocial individualism nor a closed biological family unit.

This qualification strengthens rather than destroys Fukuyama's central insight. Kinship is an important infrastructure of cooperation, but it is not the only one. It can help communities coordinate care, production, defence, and mutual assistance. At the same time, obligations to relatives can conflict with rules attached to public offices. Fukuyama uses the expression “tyranny of cousins” to capture the pressure placed on officeholders to distribute resources or appointments through kin networks rather than impersonal criteria.[^11] The philosophical point is not that family loyalty is inherently irrational or immoral. It is that a rule-bound public institution asks an official to distinguish obligations of office from obligations of personal relationship. Political development does not eliminate human sociality; it reorganizes and channels competing forms of it.

Fukuyama's anthropology is therefore probabilistic rather than strictly deterministic. Evolved dispositions create tendencies and recurring problems, not fixed institutional outcomes. Kin preference does not mechanically produce patrimonial government, and norm-following does not guarantee rule of law. Similar human capacities can support different institutions because norms, environments, technologies, conflicts, and historical sequences vary. This point is essential to the governing argument of the present study: Fukuyama's anthropology helps identify recurring institutional problems, but it does not by itself prove a universal route through which those problems must be solved.

The account also changes how the classical state of nature should be approached. If human beings were always socially embedded, an empirical story of isolated individuals would be implausible. Yet the state of nature in Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau does not perform one uniform empirical function. A fair comparison must distinguish anthropological assumptions from structural, juridical, conjectural, and normative purposes.

## 3.4 The State of Nature: Fukuyama in Contrast with Hobbes, Locke, and Rousseau

The state of nature is not a single doctrine shared by Hobbes, Locke, Rousseau, and Fukuyama. All four address relations among human nature, social order, conflict, cooperation, and political authority, but they do so through different methods. Hobbes examines the structural insecurity that follows when no common power can enforce agreements. Locke describes a moral and juridical condition of free and equal persons under natural law but without an impartial common judge. Rousseau constructs a conjectural genealogy to distinguish natural capacities from traits produced by social dependence. Fukuyama proposes an empirical and comparative reconstruction based on evolutionary biology, anthropology, and history.[^12]

The comparison is nevertheless philosophically useful if its limits are observed. It can show which assumptions about human sociability are open to empirical correction, which problems political authority is intended to solve, and which conclusions depend on normative rather than historical premises. It becomes misleading only when the four accounts are treated as rival scientific reports about a single prehistoric condition.

### 3.4.1 Hobbes and the State of Nature as War

Hobbes begins from rough equality rather than from the claim that human beings are identical. Even the physically weaker can threaten the stronger through planning or alliance, while individuals commonly hope to attain their ends. In the absence of a common authority, this equality generates insecurity through competition, diffidence, and the pursuit of reputation. The state of war does not mean uninterrupted physical combat. It describes a condition in which the known willingness to use force makes reliable security impossible.[^13]

This account is often simplified into the view that human beings are naturally solitary and wholly selfish. That interpretation overlooks the laws of nature developed by Hobbes. Reason directs persons to seek peace when it can be obtained, to keep valid covenants, and to accept conditions necessary for cooperation. The problem is not the complete absence of reason or cooperative desire. It is the absence of an authority capable of making compliance sufficiently secure. A person who performs an agreement while others defect may expose himself to destruction. The covenant establishing a commonwealth therefore authorizes a representative power able to enforce peace and defence.[^14]

Fukuyama is right to challenge any literal picture of human prehistory populated by detached individuals. Evolved human beings lived in social groups and possessed cooperative capacities before formal states. Yet this anthropological point does not remove Hobbes's structural question. Groups as well as individuals can confront insecurity when no superior authority can guarantee agreements. Hobbes's argument can therefore survive the rejection of presocial individualism because its central concern is the relation between enforceable authority and stable peace, not a scientific chronology of the first human community.

### 3.4.2 Locke and the State of Nature as Rational Freedom

Locke's state of nature differs fundamentally from Hobbes's state of war. It is a condition of freedom and equality governed by natural law. Individuals are morally obligated not to harm others in their life, liberty, health, or possessions, and each possesses a limited executive power to punish violations.[^15] The state of nature can therefore contain moral duties, family relations, exchange, cooperation, and property. The state of war arises when force is used without right; it is not identical with natural freedom.

Property illustrates the normative purpose of Locke's construction. Individuals may appropriate from the common world through labour, subject to moral limits, before the creation of political government. Political society is later formed by consent because the enjoyment of rights remains insecure where individuals judge and enforce their own cases. Government remedies the lack of settled public law, impartial adjudication, and reliable enforcement. Its authority is consequently limited by the purposes for which people entrust power to it.[^16]

Fukuyama's critique of the presocial individual must therefore be qualified when applied to Locke. Locke's persons are not without morality or every social relation before political society. His distinction is between natural and political authority, not necessarily between isolation and all community. Evolutionary anthropology may show that real human beings never began as independent proprietors deliberating outside social institutions. It does not by itself answer Locke's juridical question: under what conditions can one person acquire legitimate political authority over another? Fukuyama supplies a genealogy of institutional emergence, while Locke supplies an argument concerning rights, consent, and political trust.

### 3.4.3 Rousseau and the Noble Savage

The inherited heading “the noble savage” is useful only as shorthand. Rousseau does not simply describe an innocent prehistoric society and present it as verified history. In the *Discourse on the Origin and Foundations of Inequality*, he explicitly proceeds through conjecture in order to separate original human capacities from qualities acquired through social development.[^17] Natural human beings are depicted as relatively solitary, guided by self-preservation and pity, and lacking many developed moral, linguistic, and comparative relations. This condition is neither Hobbesian war nor a complete political ideal.

Rousseau's central concern is transformation. Perfectibility permits human capacities and forms of dependence to change. The development of cooperation, tools, settled relations, comparison, property, and social esteem alters both material life and psychology. Inequality becomes politically significant when institutions stabilize dependence and allow advantages to be converted into durable domination. In the *Discourse*, the apparent political compact may protect the holdings of the rich rather than institute legitimate freedom. A genealogy of political society is therefore not automatically a justification of it.

Fukuyama's evolutionary evidence places real pressure on a literal claim that early humans normally lived in complete solitude. His criticism is nevertheless incomplete if Rousseau's conjecture is treated only as an obsolete anthropological report. Rousseau abstracts from developed society to ask which features of inequality and dependence are natural and which are historically produced. The empirical credibility of particular stages matters, but the philosophical force of the argument also lies in exposing contingency. Ironically, this aim overlaps with Fukuyama's own insistence that contemporary institutions are historical achievements rather than direct expressions of an unchanging nature.

### 3.4.4 Fukuyama and the Evolutionary State of Nature

Fukuyama replaces the image of solitary beginnings with an evolutionary genealogy of socially embedded humans. Family and small-group organization precede centralized political authority. Cooperation is supported by kinship, reciprocity, shared norms, reputation, religion, and status relations. Larger identities and organizations emerge historically rather than through a single original social contract. On this account, the state is not created at one moment by independent individuals; it develops through multiple pressures that include conflict, religion, economic change, administration, leadership, and environmental conditions.[^18]

This approach allows Fukuyama to distinguish family or band organization from tribal organization. He treats tribes as historically constructed lineage systems rather than the timeless original form of humanity. Agriculture, ancestor worship, religious belief, and increased social scale can extend or reshape the language of kinship. Yet the terminology requires qualification. Contemporary political anthropology questions the use of “band,” “tribe,” “chiefdom,” and “state” as steps on a universal ladder. The categories can still identify differences in scale, hierarchy, administration, and authority, but they should function as revisable ideal types rather than mandatory stages.

Fukuyama also recognizes that statelessness is not equivalent to total disorder. Tribal societies may possess property rules, customary law, mechanisms of retaliation, and organized violence without centralized government.[^19] This point complicates an easy opposition between a Hobbesian chaos and the state. It suggests that the decisive institutional question is not whether social order exists at all, but how authority is organized, how widely rules apply, and whether coercion and administration are centralized. The transition to a state involves a new concentration of territorial authority and hierarchy, not the first appearance of every norm or cooperative practice.

The strongest part of Fukuyama's alternative is therefore empirical and classificatory. Humans are naturally social; kinship is neither an optional contract nor a remnant external to politics; and political organization develops through collective processes rather than isolated choice. The weaker part is the inference from recurrent problems to broad developmental transitions. Warfare, religion, and kinship reorganization can contribute to political scaling, but none is necessary or sufficient in every case. Current anthropology supports a plural-mechanism account more readily than a universal evolutionary sequence.

### 3.4.5 Comparative Analysis

The four accounts can now be compared without treating them as interchangeable histories. Hobbes asks why peace remains insecure without a common enforcing authority. Locke asks how legitimate political authority can arise among persons who are already morally free and equal. Rousseau asks how historically acquired dependence and inequality come to appear natural. Fukuyama asks how evolved social beings actually developed increasingly complex political institutions. Their questions overlap, but their conclusions are supported by different kinds of reasons.

Fukuyama's evidence most clearly defeats a literal anthropology of naturally isolated individuals. It also shows that kinship, norms, cooperation, and group identity must be incorporated into any credible genealogy of political institutions. This correction matters because theories that begin from independent individuals may conceal the inherited social structures through which real people act. It is especially valuable for explaining why public institutions repeatedly confront personal and kin-based obligations.

The correction does not, however, refute the classical arguments as a whole. Hobbes's relationship between common authority and secure compliance can be reconstructed without a prehistoric population of solitary adults. Locke's natural condition establishes moral equality and the limits of legitimate government, not merely a chronological report. Rousseau's conjectural method tests the contingency of inequality and social psychology. Empirical evidence can challenge assumptions embedded in these arguments, but their normative conclusions must be assessed through their own premises concerning authority, rights, freedom, and legitimacy.

The comparison therefore yields a two-sided result. Fukuyama improves the empirical account of human sociality and places kinship at the centre of political development. The contract and conjectural traditions, however, disclose questions his genealogy cannot answer by itself. An explanation of how the state emerged does not establish when its coercive power is legitimate. A description of evolved cooperation does not determine which laws are just. The historical development of accountability does not by itself specify who ought to count as the people or what rulers owe them.

This distinction prepares the inquiry into the institutions of state. Chapter Four can draw on Fukuyama's genealogy to examine the mechanisms through which centralized authority, autonomous law, and political accountability developed. It must also retain the philosophical lesson of the present comparison: institutional origins, institutional functions, and normative justification are related questions, but they are not the same question.
